What she was told

«Бьёт — значит любит»“If he hits you, he loves you.”

«Ұят болады!»“It would be shameful!”

«Ыдыс-аяқ сылдырламай, үй болмайды»“There is no home where dishes don’t clatter.”

These are the replies almost every woman in Kazakhstan heard when she cried for help. Domestic violence remains one of the country’s most significant social problems, and it stays silent, held in place by social norms and underreporting. (Kainazarova et al., 2025)

In 2017, the article on “infliction of minor bodily harm” was decriminalized and moved into administrative law. Punishment for domestic violence fell to fines or warnings.

The tradition

A new family. An old sentence.

Married women, hoping to build a life with the people they love, too often become captives of an old and abusive tradition. Proverbs do much of the work.

«Қайтып келген қыз жаман» — “a daughter who returns home is no good.”

Together with “Ұят болады,” sayings like this push women to endure. Many believe every woman suffers the same behind her own door, and so they say nothing, afraid of public judgement. A patriarchal society made women its victims, and then justified what it did to them.

What is reported

68,000, and “in reality the numbers are much higher.”

In 2021, more than 68,000 cases were officially reported to police. The social project Schitaetsya.kz built this image because words, statistics and histograms are easy to scroll past. A bruise is not.

The image names Oskemen as the city with the most reports. Possible causes cited include “low socio-economic development, high level of poverty, low level education” (Mussabekova et al., 2024). But a high count can also mean a place with better reporting and better support. Silence looks like safety on a chart.

Research question

How do domestic violence reporting and access to support systems vary across Kazakhstan’s regions?

This study examines legal assistance, state protection of victims, and how support systems are distributed across regions. Kazakhstan is a vast country of large cities and remote rural districts, and the protection available to a woman depends on where she lives. A woman in Astana and a woman in a rural district are not equally likely to be heard, believed, or sheltered.

In 2017, Almaty region recorded 464 cases, Almaty city 434, Astana 418 and Karaganda region 395. In 2025, the preliminary regional table recorded 711 in Shymkent, 634 in Almaty region, and 618 each in Astana and Almaty city.

These are absolute numbers, not prevalence rates. Larger populations produce larger counts even when no individual woman is at higher risk. A low number may also mean a woman had nowhere to go to be counted.

To compare regions fairly, the project proposes calculating recorded cases per 100,000 female residents, using regional population statistics for each year. Even then, that measures what was recorded, not what happened.

Measuring access to support

A recorded crime is not a rescue.

Since the question is about access to protection, crime statistics alone are not enough. We would also need to know where crisis centers, shelters, and legal and psychological assistance actually are.

Availability asks whether a service exists. Accessibility asks whether a person can realistically use it. A shelter may exist in a region but have no free beds. A crisis center may offer legal help but be hours away from the village where she lives. Judging accessibility means looking at location, capacity, distance, eligibility, and the kinds of help offered.

A UN-supported regional programme report looked at how crisis centers in Kazakhstan are paid for. Survey evidence on prevalence existed at the oblast level, but crisis centers were funded from city budgets. The numbers describing the need and the money meeting it were collected at different levels, so no one could easily match them.

Where does the data go silent?

What the statistics cannot say.

Official statistics are valuable, but they give only a partial picture of violence and of access to protection.

  • Many never report. Fear of retaliation, stigma, financial dependence, and not knowing what help exists all keep women quiet.
  • A registered case is not a resolved one. The count does not show whether she received effective help.
  • National and regional totals hide inequality between cities and villages.
  • A number of crisis centers is not a measure of care: it says nothing about capacity, quality, confidentiality, or whether anyone can reach them.
  • The rules keep changing. New laws and new statistical categories make years hard to compare.

These are not merely technical problems. They shape how institutions see need and where they spend. If the data cannot see the barriers, regional inequality stays difficult to measure, and what cannot be measured is easy to ignore.

This project separates what is measured, what can reasonably be inferred, and what remains unknown.

Section 3 · Current state of research

How can regional inequalities be explained?

Research falls into three complementary approaches: sociocultural, socioeconomic, and institutional or geographical. Each looks at a different mechanism affecting violence, disclosure, and access to help.

Sociocultural explanations

Gender norms, family expectations, and stigma can discourage women from disclosing abuse. Where family unity and reputation come first, help outside the household may feel like betrayal. In Kazakhstan, ұят болады is a starting point for studying how shame and social judgement shape help-seeking.

This needs caution. It must not portray Kazakh society as uniform or assume tradition inevitably produces violence. Attitudes differ across social groups and places, and studies are needed to show how.

Socioeconomic explanations

Economic dependence, scarce jobs, housing insecurity and transport costs shape whether a woman can leave or reach a professional. These conditions differ across regions, so they may matter for regional inequality.

But disadvantage is not a sufficient explanation. Violence occurs in every social group, and poverty may affect the ability to get protection without explaining why abuse happens.

Institutional and geographical explanations

Legal protection depends on more than legislation: on enforcement, administrative resources, professional capacity, and available services. A law can apply to the whole country while real access to help varies from one community to the next.

The 2024 legal reforms are a chance to ask whether changes in criminal law were followed by better access to services. Legal reform and service provision should be evaluated separately.

The research gap

The three approaches show that protection has social, economic and institutional dimensions. Yet how these relate to regional service accessibility has barely been studied. The provisional gap is the limited visibility of comparable regional information linking officially recorded violence to where support services are and whether they can be reached. This gap still has to be verified in the academic literature rather than assumed.

The project does not claim causation from descriptive statistics. It aims to identify patterns, limitations, and questions that need further evidence.

Section 4 · The AI-assisted solution and its costs

Qorgau AI

Know your options. Choose your next step.

A proposed AI-assisted service that gives information and connects people to verified domestic violence support resources. It does not replace counselors or lawyers.

1. How would it work?

Imagine a woman in a rural area who is being abused. She does not trust the police and does not know where to go. She opens the Qorgau website and chooses her region, her language, and the kind of information she needs.

ILLUSTRATIVE WEBSITE PROTOTYPE
How can we help you?

You can explore information without creating an account. This is a concept demonstration, not a live support service.

YOUR SELECTED PATHWAY
The proposed service would retrieve verified legal information, explain procedures in plain language, and offer access to a qualified professional. This prototype does not connect to real organizations, collect reports, or provide crisis intervention.

2. Where does AI actually contribute?

  • Multilingual explanation. Translate and simplify verified legal and service information in Kazakh and Russian, with human review.
  • Information retrieval. Answer questions from a controlled database of verified services and official guidance, rather than generating unsupported legal advice.
  • Service navigation. Help users find relevant options from their region and stated needs, without profiling them or predicting their risk.

The system must not classify anyone as a victim, assign a danger score, contact police, or send sensitive conversations to government databases.

3. Two sides of one problem

Public dataReported domestic violence, regional differences, trust in legal institutions, and availability of support services.
Individual supportA confidential information service that helps people navigate existing institutions without forcing an immediate decision to report.

The analysis may show that reported cases are not a straightforward measure of actual prevalence. Trust in institutions may influence reporting, though the data would have to establish whether that relationship exists. Qorgau would address one barrier only: the difficulty of finding trustworthy information and reaching appropriate help. It would not solve domestic violence, which has deeper social, economic and institutional causes.

4. What would it cost?

The proposal is independent civil-society oversight, minimal data collection, no mandatory accounts, and human-reviewed information. A visible quick-exit feature could help, but it would not erase browser history or guarantee safety on a monitored device. The strongest form of the argument may be that government should fund the service without having unrestricted access to users' conversations.

Case study: AinoAid

AinoAid was developed through the European IMPROVE project: a multilingual, AI-assisted platform on domestic violence, support options and legal procedures, combining a chatbot with an expert-built knowledge base. Its published evaluation included interviews with 80 survivors across five European countries and a usability survey of 669 users in Germany. Users rated usability and perceived safety positively. The study also found limits in empathy, in understanding user input, and in giving guidance people could act on.

Good usability scores do not show that the technology reduces violence or improves long-term outcomes for survivors. So the Kazakhstan platform would take a narrower role: verified public information, not automated personal counseling.

Who should pay?

A partnership: a public funding body paying for the pilot, with independent civil-society organizations and qualified providers controlling service standards, privacy safeguards and referral procedures. The separation matters. Survivors should not have to confess to a government-run chatbot just to learn what support exists.

Data ethics

Collect only what the purpose needs. Label AI output clearly and have humans review it. Never present incomplete regional statistics as proof that some communities are inherently more violent or less safe. The central risk is that an effort to improve access to information creates new misinformation, stigma, or surveillance.

₸30.36 million is a defensible illustrative figure, not an empirically verified estimate. Salaries, hosting, audits and comparable projects still need documented sources, and the capacity of existing support organizations to take more referrals has not been confirmed.

Final verdict

Only under conditions. And no substitute for the real thing.

Kazakhstan could consider piloting Qorgau AI, but only on strict terms: a verified service directory, human-reviewed answers, independent security testing, and clear escalation to qualified professionals. Expansion should depend on evidence that it improves access without raising safety risks.

Its value lies in organizing fragmented public information and making regional gaps in support more visible. But AI has to be justified by demonstrated benefit, not novelty. If a plain database and interactive map can do the same job more accurately, safely and cheaply, the non-AI option is better. AI belongs only where it has a clear edge, such as multilingual document processing under human supervision.

Any version needs verified information, institutional accountability, funding for maintenance, and safeguards against collecting or exposing personal data.

A digital platform cannot replace shelters, trained professionals, legal protection, or public investment in support services.

AI may help identify and communicate regional information gaps. It is not a substitute for the institutional resources that domestic violence demands.
